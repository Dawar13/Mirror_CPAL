\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Learned symmetry in trained networks.}
Equivariance can be built into an architecture, as in group-equivariant CNNs~\citep{cohen2016group}, or learned from
data. Networks trained on natural images often contain families of units related by a transformation, such as rotated
or mirrored curve detectors in InceptionV1~\citep{olah2020naturally}, and their representations can be
measured for equivariance directly~\citep{lenc2015understanding,gruver2023lie}.
\citet{bokman2023investigating} showed that CNNs trained to be flip-invariant are close to group-equivariant CNNs whose
channels permute under a flip, and merged such a network with its own mirrored copy.
\citet{shahverdi2026identifiable} prove that, under identifiability conditions, an equivariant network must be
equivariant layer by layer, and observe this in small models trained from scratch. We study released vision
transformers, where the residual stream needs a learned mirror operator with odd directions rather than a channel
permutation, we compare four symmetries in the same models, and we turn the structure into a smaller network.

\paragraph{Making pretrained models equivariant.}
Equivariance can be added to a pretrained model by averaging over the group at inference or during
fine-tuning~\citep{basu2023equi,basu2023efficient}, by learning a canonicalisation of the
input~\citep{kaba2023equivariance,mondal2023equivariant}, or by projecting the weights onto a designed equivariant
subspace and fine-tuning~\citep{rahman2026tunable}. Group averaging multiplies the inference cost by the group size.
Mirror Surgery instead estimates the mirror operator and the pairing of units from the model's own activations,
gives exact rather than approximate equivariance, and reduces the parameter count rather than increasing the cost.
Mirror-equivariant vision transformers can also be trained from scratch with fewer FLOPs~\citep{bokman2025flopping};
our method needs no retraining.

\paragraph{Structured compression.}
Post-training compression of transformers removes parameters by low-rank factorisation
(plain SVD, activation-aware SVD~\citep{yuan2023asvd} and SVD-LLM~\citep{wang2025svdllm}), by slicing a rotated
residual stream~\citep{ashkboos2024slicegpt}, by structured pruning~\citep{an2024flap}, or by merging correlated
units~\citep{stoica2024zipit}. Token merging~\citep{bolya2023tome} reduces the number of tokens instead. These methods
look for redundancy in the statistics of the activations. The symmetry tax is a different kind of redundancy: the two
units of a twin pair respond to mirrored inputs, so they are almost uncorrelated on any single image and invisible to
correlation-based merging. We compare against these methods at equal parameter counts and also test whether the two
kinds of redundancy stack.
