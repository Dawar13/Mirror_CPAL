# Reference verification for paper/reference.bib

Checked on 17 November 2026. Every entry in `paper/reference.bib` is listed below with the source that was opened and what it confirmed. Nothing in the .bib file rests on memory or on the earlier project notes alone.

## Summary

- 69 entries in `paper/reference.bib`.
- VERIFIED: 65. CORRECTED: 4. NOT FOUND, excluded: 0.
- Corrected entries: `simeoni2025dinov3` (DINOv3 is published in TMLR 2026), `ong2026inherent` (arXiv preprint, not a NeurIPS 2026 main-track paper), `santosescriche2026how` (the NeurIPS 2026 version has a different title, so it is cited as arXiv), `ma2022principles` (publisher field and DOI).
- Keys follow the ones already used in `paper/sections/*.tex`, so the draft compiles without edits. Every key the draft cites is in the file. One key carries a year that differs from the entry: `simeoni2025dinov3` is the TMLR 2026 version (the arXiv version is from August 2025).
- The file compiles with BibTeX and `unsrtnat` with no warnings (tested on a scratch copy with `\nocite{*}`).

## How this was checked

- **arXiv export API** for titles, full author lists, version dates, comments and journal-ref fields (all arXiv IDs below).
- **Crossref API** for publisher DOI records (journals, IEEE CVPR and ICVGIP proceedings, AAAI, Springer LNCS chapters): authors, venue, volume, issue, pages, year.
- **Proceedings pages**: PMLR (ICML 2016, 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026), CVF Open Access (bibref blocks), NeurIPS proceedings (citation metadata and two PDFs), proceedings.iclr.cc (2024, 2025, 2026), iclr.cc virtual pages (ICLR 2021 and 2023), neurips.cc virtual pages (2026), ECVA (ECCV 2022).
- **OpenReview API search** for venue and decision fields (TMLR acceptance, rejected submissions). The search also returns DBLP-imported records, which stood in for DBLP itself.
- **PubMed** (E-utilities) for the natural image statistics abstracts; author-hosted PDFs for full text where available (Torralba and Oliva; Simoncelli and Olshausen).
- **Dataset pages**: Caltech CUB-200-2011, Toronto CIFAR, Zenodo (Roy and Vetterli).
- Blocked and therefore not used: DBLP web and API (bot check), OpenReview web pages (bot check; the API worked), SpringerLink (bot check). Where a detail could only have come from a blocked source it was left out (see `touvron2022deit`).
- Policy for page numbers: CVF papers from 2021 onwards use the CVF Open Access page range and no DOI; older CVPR papers use the IEEE DOI and the IEEE page range, which for CVPR 2017 differs from the numbering printed in the CVF PDF. ICLR entries carry no pages, as is usual. TMLR entries carry the OpenReview URL.

## Natural image statistics: what each paper supports

The paper wants to argue that natural images are, on average, left-right symmetric and anisotropic between horizontal and vertical orientations. Here is what the four papers actually say.

**Horizontal and vertical anisotropy of power spectra: supported by Torralba and Oliva (2003).**

- Section 2.1, p. 393: "Related studies found a bias in the distribution of orientations, illustrated in the power spectra of figure 2. In real-world images, including both natural landscapes and man-made environments, vertical and horizontal orientations are more frequent than obliques (Baddeley 1997, Switkes et al 1978, van der Schaaf and van Hateren 1996, Oliva and Torralba 2001)."
- Same page: "Table 1 shows that the values of the slope α and the amplitude A vary with orientation and also with the type of environment."
- p. 397: "As distance grows, there is an increased bias towards vertical and horizontal orientations, together with the point of view of the observer becoming more constrained. As distance continues to increase, energy is concentrated mainly in vertical spatial frequencies, as very large environmental scenes are organized along horizontal layers."

**Orientation dependence (direction not stated in the abstract): van der Schaaf and van Hateren (1996).** Abstract (PubMed): "Both the total power in a spectrum (corresponding to image contrast) and its dependence on spatial frequency vary considerably between images, and also within images when considered as functions of orientation." and "Most information is gained from contrast, 1/f2 spatial frequency behaviour, and contrast as a function of orientation." The full text (Elsevier) was not read, so the claim that it shows a horizontal and vertical excess rests on Torralba and Oliva citing it (verify in the full text before citing it alone for that point).

**1/f² power spectrum, averaged over orientation: Simoncelli and Olshausen (2001).** p. 1202: "The two-dimensional power spectrum can then be reduced to a one-dimensional function of spatial frequency by performing a rotational average within the two-dimensional Fourier plane. Empirically, many authors have found that the spectral power of natural images falls with frequency, f, according to a power law, 1/f^p, with estimated values for p typically near 2". This review does not discuss orientation anisotropy, so do not cite it for that.

**Field (1987).** Only the abstract was read (PubMed); it does not mention orientation anisotropy or a 1/f law. Torralba and Oliva (p. 393) cite it among the "seminal studies" for the 1/f^α spectrum with α near 2. Citing Field (1987) for the 1/f spectrum is standard, but it was not checked in the full text here (verify). Do not cite it for anisotropy.

**Left-right symmetry: none of the four papers states it.** Torralba and Oliva support the opposite point for the vertical axis, which is useful for the up-down control. p. 398: "Therefore, different image statistics will characterize the top and bottom half of the image (e.g. smooth texture of the sky, long vertical contours of skylines at the top, cluttered forms at the bottom)." The claim that natural images are left-right symmetric on average needs another source or our own measurement (verify).

**A caution for the argument (our derivation, not a citation).** The power spectrum cannot tell a left-right flip from an up-down flip. For a real image with Fourier transform F(u, v), a left-right flip gives F(-u, v) and an up-down flip gives F(u, -v), and |F(-u, v)| = |F(u, -v)| because F(-u, -v) is the complex conjugate of F(u, v). So the horizontal and vertical anisotropy of power spectra says nothing about left-right versus up-down mirror symmetry. That argument has to rest on position-dependent or higher-order statistics, such as the top-bottom difference in Torralba and Oliva's windowed spectra (their Figure 6).
